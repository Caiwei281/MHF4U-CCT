Cosine graph

Transformation

I chose this photo taken in Subang Jaya, Malaysia, because it captures a fun, casual moment with my friend, and the smooth, wavy curve formed by our bodies lying back‑to‑back perfectly matches the shape of a cosine function. I started with the parent function y = cos(x), which has a regular, symmetrical wave shape. Comparing it to the curve in the image, I observed the wave was vertically stretched, shifted horizontally to the right, and translated upward — no reflection was needed, as the curve rose and fell in the same direction as the original cosine graph.

At first, it was difficult to determine the vertical stretch factor because the curve was not positioned near the origin, making its amplitude hard to measure accurately. To simplify, I mentally shifted the curve so its midline aligned with the x‑axis, allowing me to focus only on its height and shape without the complexity of translations. By comparing the maximum and minimum heights, I determined a vertical stretch by a factor of 2 fit best, and refined this value until the curve closely overlapped the outline of our bodies in the photo.

Once the amplitude was set, I moved the curve back into its correct position: shifted 1 unit to the right to align the peak of the wave with the highest point of the curve, then shifted 3 units upward to centre the wave properly. This resulted in my final function: y = 2cos(x - 1) + 3

Summary of Transformations: - Vertical stretch by a factor of 2 → 2cos(x) - Horizontal translation 1 unit to the right → 2cos(x - 1) - Vertical translation 3 units upward → 2cos(x - 1) + 3

Ideally, I would have captured the full periodic wave to model its complete shape, but this domain covers only the section shown — so the model applies only to this visible part.

The parent function y = cos(x) has a domain D: {x∈R} x ∈ ℝ and range R: { y∈R|−1≤y≤1 } ; it is defined for all real x-values and oscillates continuously between -1 and 1, with no asymptotes or discontinuities. My transformed function also has no undefined points, and within this restricted domain, the curve remains fully continuous — so I do not need to consider discontinuities when describing the domain and range for this situation.

Since the cosine function is smooth and continuous over this entire interval, the range can be determined by evaluating the function at the domain’s endpoints and its maximum/minimum values. The lowest values are approximately 1.00–1.32, and the highest value at the peak (1,5) is 5.00, giving an overall range of approximately: R:{ y ∈ ℝ 1 . 00 ≤ y ≤ 5 . 00 }

Polynomial Function-Quartic Function

Transformation

I chose this photograph taken in Subang Jaya, Malaysia, because the smooth, symmetrical arch formed when my friend and I joined our hands overhead creates a perfect downward‑opening curve — ideal for modelling with a fourth‑degree polynomial with multiple terms. I began with the parent quartic function y = x^4, which forms a narrow, upward‑opening curve. Comparing it to the image, the arch is much wider, opens downward, sits far below the origin, and spans a clear horizontal distance and vertical height — this told me a reflection, vertical compression, and downward shift were needed to match its shape and position.

At first, it was tricky to determine the compression factor because the curve was far from the origin, making its steepness hard to measure. To simplify, I mentally moved the curve so its highest point sat on the x-axis, letting me focus only on its width and curvature. I found that compressing vertically by a factor of 1/30 and reflecting across the x-axis gave the correct gentle downward curve, resulting in y = −1/30x^4 . I then shifted the entire curve 5 units downward so its highest point aligned exactly with our joined hands in the photo. This gave my final function: y = −1/30x^4−5

Summary of Transformations Reflection across the x-axis: y = −x^4 Vertical compression by a factor of 1/30 : y = −1/30x^4 Vertical translation 5 units downward: y = −1/30x^4−5 − 1 / 30 x^ 4 − 5

Domain and Range

The domain of my function, defined by the total horizontal distance covered by the visible arch in the photograph, is: D: {x∈R|−4.228≤x≤4.234} This interval spans approximately 8.46 units horizontally, matching the full width of the arch from one side to the other. Ideally, I would have captured the full extent of the curve, but this restricted domain accurately represents only the portion visible in the image — so the model applies only to this section.

The parent polynomial function y =x^4 has a domain D: {x∈R} and range R: {y∈R|y≥0} ; polynomials are defined and continuous for all real x-values, with no asymptotes, gaps, or discontinuities. My transformed polynomial function is also defined everywhere, and within this restricted domain, the curve remains fully smooth and continuous — no discontinuities need to be considered when analysing this situation.

Since polynomial functions are continuous over any closed interval, the range is determined by the vertical height of the curve: the lowest y-value is -15.8 at both outer ends of the arch, and the highest value at the vertex (0,-5) is exactly -5, giving a total vertical range of 10.8 units. Therefore, the range of my function is: R: {y∈R|−15.8≤y≤−5} y ∈ ℝ − 15 . 8 ≤ y ≤ − 5

Tan graph

Transformation

I chose this fun photo taken in Subang Jaya, Malaysia, because the smooth, sweeping curve my friend and I made while lying side‑by‑side looks exactly like the shape of a tangent graph — it’s such a cool way to turn our relaxed pose into a math model! I started by looking at the parent tangent function, y = tan(x). It has a very distinct shape: it rises steeply from left to right, repeats every 180 degree, and has vertical asymptotes — invisible lines where the graph shoots upward or downward and is not defined.

Compared to that parent graph, the curve we formed was much gentler, not nearly as steep, taller overall, shifted to the right, and sat lower down. So I adjusted the parent function step‑by‑step to match perfectly. First, I stretched it vertically by a factor of 5 — this made it taller and less steep, matching the gentle slope of our bodies. Next, I shifted the whole graph 2 units to the right so its centre lined up exactly with the highest point of our curve. Finally, I moved it 2 units downward so it sat at the correct height to trace our pose. The final function I ended up with is: y = 5tan(x - 2) - 2 Summary of Transformations - Vertical stretch by a factor of 5: y = 5tan(x) - Horizontal translation 2 units right: y = 5tan(x - 2) - Vertical translation 2 units down: y = 5tan(x - 2) - 2

Domain and Range

I titled my graph Body Length as a Function of Horizontal Distance Between the Two Students. The x‑axis represents the horizontal distance between us, measured in units, while the y‑axis shows the corresponding vertical height or length of our bodies forming the curve.

Looking again at the parent tangent function y = tan(x): its domain includes all real numbers except x = π/2+nπ , where those vertical asymptotes occur — at these points, the function has no defined value and is discontinuous. Its range covers all real numbers from negative infinity to positive infinity, meaning it can go infinitely low or infinitely high.

For my transformed function, I only used the smooth, continuous section visible in the photo — my restricted domain is: D: {x∈R|−7.04≤x≤2.38|} This spans 9.42 horizontal units, and importantly — the asymptotes of the tangent graph lie far outside this interval — so there are no breaks, gaps, or undefined values to worry about in my model. Unlike the parent function that stretches infinitely, the range of my graph is limited to the actual height of our curve: R: {y∈R|−16≤y≤7|} This covers 23 vertical units, from the lowest point of our pose all the way up to its highest point — perfectly matching how our bodies curve upward and then back down.

Titled Body Length as a Function of Horizontal Distance Between the Two Students, this graph uses y = 5tan(x - 2) - 2 to model our pose. Compared to the steep, infinitely extending parent tangent function y = tan(x), my transformed version is gentler, shifted, and restricted to a domain from -7.04 to 2.38 and a range from -16 to 7. This shows how simple changes can adapt a parent graph to fit real‑world shapes perfectly!
